Self Care Matters: Why Mental and Somatic Care Keep You Balanced

2026-09-11 · 11 min read · By Ashleigh Duncan, Counsellor MBACP

From a mental health view, self care helps protect your mood, attention, boundaries, and sense of meaning. From a somatic view, self care also includes the body: breath, movement, posture, rest, touch, digestion, and the felt sense of safety.

The mind and body do not operate in separate rooms. Anxiety can tighten the chest. Grief can drain the limbs. Chronic stress can show up as headaches, jaw tension, stomach upset, shallow breathing, or fatigue. Caring for one part of you affects the whole system.

This article is informational and not a substitute for mental health or medical care. If distress feels intense, ongoing, or unsafe, support from a licensed professional matters.

Self care begins with small moments of honest attention.

Self care is a nervous system practice

Self care matters because stress is not only a thought. It is a full-body event.

When the brain senses pressure, danger, conflict, or overload, the nervous system prepares the body to respond. Heart rate may rise. Muscles may brace. Breath may get faster. Attention may narrow. This response can help in short bursts. It becomes draining when it never gets a clear signal that the threat has passed.

That is where somatic care comes in. “Somatic” simply means related to the body. Somatic self care asks, “What is my body experiencing right now, and what would help it feel a little more supported?”

This does not mean you have to stay calm all the time. That would be unrealistic. It means you build moments of regulation into your day so your body can move in and out of stress instead of getting stuck there.

Common signs that your system may need care include:

  • Trouble taking a full breath
  • A tight jaw, neck, belly, or shoulders
  • Racing thoughts at night
  • Feeling numb, detached, or foggy
  • Snapping at people over small things
  • Feeling tired but wired
  • Needing constant distraction
  • Losing interest in things that usually help

These signs are not failures. They are information. The body often speaks before the mind has words.

“Self care is how you tell your nervous system, over and over, that you are allowed to be safe.”

That message does not always come from a big life change. It often comes from small repeated cues: a longer exhale, unclenching your hands, stepping outside, drinking water, naming a feeling, asking for help, or going to bed before you crash.

Mental self care helps you make sense of what you carry

Mental self care includes the practices that help you relate to your thoughts and emotions with more clarity. It does not mean forcing positive thoughts or acting like everything is fine. It means making room to notice what is true without letting every thought run the day.

The mind can create loops. A single worry can turn into a story about the future. A hard conversation can replay for hours. A mistake can become proof that you are not doing well enough. When this happens, mental self care helps you pause and sort the facts from the fear.

A simple practice is the

name and narrow

technique.

Start by naming what is happening in plain language:

“I am having a worry thought.”

“I feel embarrassed.”

“My mind is predicting the worst.”

“I am tired and taking things personally.”

Then narrow your focus to the next kind action:

  • Send one message.
  • Eat something steady.
  • Take three slow breaths.
  • Write down the concern and return to it later.
  • Step away from the conversation until your body settles.

This works because the mind often calms when it has a clear job. Not a perfect plan. Just the next grounded step.

Another helpful mental self care habit is setting

attention boundaries

. The mind needs recovery time from constant input. News, messages, noise, and comparison can keep the brain on alert. A boundary may look like leaving your phone in another room for 20 minutes, turning off nonessential alerts, or choosing one part of the day when you do not consume heavy information.

None of this is about avoiding life. It is about choosing when and how you meet it.

Somatic self care brings you back into the body

Many people try to think their way out of stress. Sometimes that helps. Other times the body needs a body-based signal.

Somatic techniques are useful because they work with sensation. They give the nervous system concrete cues of safety, orientation, movement, and release. They can be especially helpful when you feel anxious, frozen, scattered, or disconnected.

Try orienting when you feel overwhelmed

Orienting means slowly looking around and letting your eyes take in the space you are in. This simple act can remind the nervous system that you are here, now, and not inside the threat your mind is imagining.

Try this:

  • Place both feet on the floor.
  • Slowly turn your head and look around the room.
  • Name five things you see.
  • Notice one color, one shape, and one source of light.
  • Let your eyes land on something pleasant or neutral.
  • Take one slow breath out.

You do not need to feel peaceful right away. The goal is to help your body update its sense of the present moment.

Use the longer exhale

The breath gives direct feedback to the body. When you lengthen the exhale, you can gently invite the nervous system toward rest.

Try this for one minute:

  • Inhale through the nose for a comfortable count of three or four.
  • Exhale slowly for a count of five or six.
  • Keep the breath easy, not forced.
  • Let the shoulders soften as you breathe out.

If counting makes you tense, skip the numbers. Simply make the out-breath a little longer than the in-breath.

Release tension with a body scan

A body scan helps you find areas that are holding stress. The aim is not to relax every muscle perfectly. The aim is to notice.

Move your attention through the body:

  • Forehead and eyes
  • Jaw and tongue
  • Neck and shoulders
  • Hands and fingers
  • Chest and belly
  • Hips and legs
  • Feet and toes

At each place, ask, “Can this soften by 5 percent?” Small shifts count. A 5 percent release is often more realistic than trying to melt all tension at once.

Add movement that matches your state

When the body has stress energy, stillness may feel impossible. Movement can help complete the stress cycle.

If you feel restless, try walking, shaking out your hands, stretching your calves, or doing slow squats. If you feel low or heavy, try gentle swaying, a short walk outside, or rolling your shoulders. If you feel frozen, start tiny. Wiggle your toes. Press your feet into the floor. Turn your head slowly from side to side.

The best movement is not always intense. It is the movement your system can actually receive.

Small daily habits beat rare perfect routines

A common self care trap is waiting until you have enough time, money, energy, or quiet. That can turn care into another pressure. Real self care works better when it fits into daily life.

Think in small anchors. An anchor is a brief practice attached to something you already do.

  • After brushing your teeth
  • Before checking your phone
  • After lunch
  • Before sleep
  • After a hard conversation
  • Take three slow breaths with one hand on your chest
  • Notice your feet on the floor
  • Step outside or look out a window for two minutes
  • Write one sentence about what you are feeling
  • Shake out your hands and lengthen your exhale

These habits matter because repetition teaches the body. One long rest day after months of depletion may help, but it may not restore balance on its own. Small daily cues build a more stable base.

This is where

self care

becomes less about escape and more about maintenance. Just as you charge your phone before it dies, you can support your system before it shuts down.

A balanced self care rhythm includes several kinds of care:

Physical care

Sleep, food, hydration, movement, sunlight, medical appointments, and pain support.

Emotional care

Feeling your feelings, crying when needed, talking with someone safe, journaling, therapy, and allowing joy.

Mental care

Reducing overload, focusing on one task at a time, challenging harsh self-talk, and taking breaks from constant input.

Relational care

Clear boundaries, honest conversations, asking for help, and spending time with people who feel steady.

Spiritual or meaning-based care

Prayer, meditation, nature, creative work, service, reflection, or any practice that connects you to what matters.

You do not need all of these every day. Balance comes from noticing which area has been neglected and bringing it back into the circle.

Boundaries are part of self care

Self care is not only what you add. Sometimes it is what you stop allowing.

Without boundaries, self care can become a short break before returning to the same overload. A bath will not fix a schedule that never leaves room to breathe. A meditation app will not undo the strain of saying yes when your whole body means no.

Boundaries protect your energy, attention, and capacity. They also reduce resentment. A clear limit may feel uncomfortable at first, especially if you are used to overextending yourself. Still, discomfort does not mean the boundary is wrong.

Simple boundary phrases can help:

“I cannot take that on this week.”

“I need time to think before I answer.”

“I am available for 20 minutes.”

“I want to help, but I do not have the capacity right now.”

“I need to pause this conversation and come back to it later.”

The body often responds to honest boundaries with relief. You may feel a deeper breath, looser shoulders, or less pressure in the chest. That relief is data. It tells you that your system needed protection.

Self care also includes boundaries with yourself. That might mean not replaying a conversation at midnight, not skipping meals when you are busy, or not using productivity as proof of worth.

A five minute reset can change the tone of your day

When life feels full, a five minute practice can make self care more realistic. It does not solve every problem, but it can interrupt a stress spiral and bring you back to choice.

Try this reset when you feel tense, foggy, or overloaded.

  • Minute one

Notice your body.

Sit or stand in a way that feels supported. Let your feet touch the ground. Notice the contact between your body and the chair, floor, or surface beneath you.

Ask, “What do I feel right now?”

Use simple words: tight, warm, heavy, buzzy, tired, numb, restless, open.

  • Minute two

Lengthen your exhale.

Breathe in gently. Breathe out a little longer. Repeat without strain. Let your face soften if it wants to.

  • Minute three

Orient to the room.

Look around slowly. Name a few things you see. Let your eyes find something steady, such as a plant, blanket, lamp, tree, or patch of light.

  • Minute four

Release one area.

Choose one place that feels tense. Jaw, shoulders, hands, belly, or legs. Invite a small release. If release does not happen, place a hand there and offer warmth.

  • Minute five

Choose one caring next step.

Ask, “What would support me in the next 10 minutes?”

Keep it simple:

  • Drink water.
  • Send the honest text.
  • Eat a snack.
  • Step outside.
  • Take a break from the screen.
  • Lie down with a hand on the heart.
  • Ask for help.

This type of reset builds trust with yourself. You learn that stress does not have to run unchecked. You can meet it, work with it, and respond with care.

When self care needs extra support

Self care is powerful, but it is not meant to replace support. If you feel stuck in panic, depression, trauma responses, chronic pain, disordered eating, substance misuse, or thoughts of self-harm, reach out to a licensed clinician, doctor, crisis line, or trusted support person.

Needing help does not mean you failed at self care. It may be the most caring choice available.

Somatic therapy, talk therapy, group support, trauma-informed care, medical treatment, and community connection can all play a role. Many people need a mix. The right care often includes both understanding your story and helping your body feel safer in the present.

The takeaway is to care before you crash

Self care matters because you are not a machine that can run without pause. Your mind needs space to process. Your body needs signals of safety. Your emotions need room to move. Your nervous system needs regular reminders that it can come down from alert.

Start small. Choose one mental practice and one somatic practice for the next week. Maybe you write one honest sentence each night. Maybe you take three longer exhales before opening your phone. Maybe you walk for five minutes after lunch or place a hand on your chest when anxiety rises.
